BLS Current Population Survey Analysis

Wage Gap in the United States, 2014–2024

Wages play a major role in shaping quality of life, economic opportunity, and financial security. Using data from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Current Population Survey (CPS), this project examines how earnings and employment outcomes have changed between 2014 and 2024 and whether wage gaps between demographic groups have narrowed over time.

Through four interactive visualizations, we investigate several factors that contribute to wage differences, including education, race, gender, employment rates, and minimum-wage work. Rather than looking at a single measure of inequality, these charts show how multiple characteristics can influence earnings and how those relationships have evolved over the last decade.

Our goal is to identify important trends, highlight persistent disparities, and explore whether progress has been made toward greater wage equity. Understanding these patterns can help inform conversations about labor markets, economic policy, and opportunities for workers across different demographic groups.

BLS Highlights of Women’s Earnings

From 2014 to 2024, earnings rose at every education level, but men still earned more.

Education is strongly tied to higher pay, but the gender gap does not disappear at higher levels of schooling. This chart compares median usual weekly earnings for full-time wage and salary workers age 25 and older in 2014 and 2024.

Each line connects 2014 to 2024 earnings for one education level and sex. Hover over a point to see exact median weekly earnings and the decade change.

Key takeaway

Workers with a bachelor’s degree or higher had the highest weekly earnings in both years, but the gap remained visible: women with bachelor’s degrees or higher earned $1,479 per week in 2024, compared with $1,907 for men. The women-to-men ratio improved slightly from 2014 to 2024 for most education groups, but no group reached parity.

BLS Employment-Population Ratio

Employment rates have maintained a steady gap over the last ten years.

Employment rates for both men and women increased gradually through the late 2010s, dropped sharply during the COVID-19 pandemic, and recovered in the years that followed. While employment improved for both groups, men consistently maintained higher employment rates throughout the decade.

Move the slider to reveal employment trends over time. Hover over any point to view the employment rate for a specific year.

Key takeaway

Employment rates fell dramatically during the pandemic in 2020 for both men and women. Although employment rebounded by 2024, men continued to maintain employment rates roughly 10 percentage points higher than women throughout the period.

BLS Hourly Earnings by Race and Gender

Hourly wages increased across all racial groups from 2014 to 2024.

Average hourly earnings rose steadily over the decade for workers of all racial backgrounds. Use the gender buttons to compare wage trends for men and women and explore how earnings changed across racial groups over time.

Hover over any point to view the average hourly wage for a specific year, race, and gender.

Key takeaway

Asian groups have the highest income for both Men and Women, even above White groups.

Overall Conclusion

Across all four analyses, the data shows that earnings and employment outcomes improved between 2014 and 2024, but differences across gender and demographic groups remain evident.

  • Education is linked to higher weekly earnings for both women and men, but from 2014 to 2024, women still earned less than men at every education level shown.
  • Men maintained consistently higher employment-to-population ratios throughout the decade. The gap remained steady with around 10 percent difference, even after the pandemic affected both groups.
  • Hourly wages increased for all racial groups, though wage differences across race and gender persisted over time.
  • The number of workers earning at or below the federal minimum wage declined significantly, but women continued to represent a larger share of affected workers.
  • Overall, the gender wage gap narrowed in several areas but did not disappear, suggesting that education, race, employment patterns, and occupational factors continue to influence earnings outcomes.

Data source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics , 2014 to 2024, Current Population Survey.
